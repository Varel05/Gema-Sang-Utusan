# Chapter 8: The Winged Lion's Iron Gate

The morning sun burned away the lingering valley mist below, unveiling a sprawling expanse of gray slate roofs and chimney smoke across the Duchy of Leovard. From the crest of the final ridge, the fortified city resembled a stone colossus crouching upon the rocky plain, girded by murky moats and towering double ramparts that stood cold and unyielding.

Captain Garrett pulled back on the reins of his black warhorse, halting upon the stony ridge. Behind him, five mounted scouts rode in close file, flanking the pack mule led by Kael. Garrett cast a sidelong glance toward Azariel and Mira. The silver-haired youth walked in silence, his right hand gripping the fold of his coarse woolen tunic, holding back something unseen from the outside.

"We make our entrance through the outer Artisans' Gate," Garrett said, his voice raspy from road dust. "Stay close behind my mount. Do not meet the toll wardens' eyes, and let my badge do the talking."

They descended the slope toward the outer ring road. Passing the outer timber barbican, the roar of the city swallowed them whole. The lower city's thoroughfare was paved with river stones worn slick by centuries of cartwheels. The air hung thick with the sharp stench of smithy soot, horse droppings, sour boiled cabbage, and mutton fat from cheap cookhouses lining the alleys.

Hundreds of souls jostled in the thoroughfare. Bare-chested blacksmiths hammered glowing iron on their anvils, scattering bright sparks across the cobbles. Grain merchants shouted prices over sacks of wheat, while draymen cracked their whips to scatter street curs squabbling over fish guts. For the common folk of Leovard, it was merely another morning driven by coin and bread. Not a single soul among them had any inkling of the horror that had just smothered the northern pass in frozen ash.

Garrett watched those oblivious faces with a bitter ache in his chest. Deep within his saddlebag, the small leather shoe of the salt merchant's child still lay tucked away, cold and still. How many of these townsfolk would survive if that violet mist spilled down the mountain cliffs and swept the lower ward before the castle gate could drop its iron grate?

The company crossed the arched stone bridge spanning the inner moat. Before them rose the grand portal of the ducal citadel: a cavernous archway of black granite beneath a four-tiered watchtower. Carved deep into the keystone above the arch was the heraldry of Leovard, a winged lion clasping an upright broadsword. Suspended beneath it hung a portcullis weighing thousands of stones, poised to drop at the release of an iron lever.

A dozen guards in polished breastplates barred the way with crossed poleaxes. Their cloaks were dyed crimson with gilded fringe, the pristine dress of an inner garrison that had never tasted the mud of the frontier.

A pot-bellied gate sergeant stepped forward, clutching a wooden ledger clamped with freshly inked parchment. His eyes narrowed insolently as he took in Garrett's warhorse and the rugged travelers clad in frontier leather behind him.

"Hold where you stand," the sergeant barked through his nose. "State your unit and business."

Garrett leaned down in the saddle, sweeping back his service cloak to reveal the silver winged-lion badge pinned upon his gorget. "Captain Garrett, commander of the northern ravine border patrol. Clear the gate, Sergeant. I bear an urgent dispatch that must reach His Grace the Duke before midday."

The sergeant did not yield an inch. His stubby fingers tapped upon his parchment. "The Border Guard holds right of passage, Captain. Yet a new decree was issued two hours ago from the chancellery. All parties arriving from the northern defile without registered merchant seals are to be held in the outer ward for pestilence inspection and travel permit verification."

Garrett's jaw locked hard. "Pestilence inspection? Two of my troopers lie dead on the cliffs and an entire salt caravan was butchered by horrors, and you dare stall me with paperwork?"

"Not my affair, Captain," the sergeant replied coldly, unflinching. "This order bears the seal of the Chancellor of the Treasury, Master Aldric. A representative of the frontier vintners had audience earlier this morning. He warned the council that lawless unrest in the outer hamlets risks bringing disease into these walls. If you take issue with the directive, lodge your complaint with the treasury desk."

Garrett's fist clenched the reins until the boiled leather groaned under his grip. Vane. The man moved like poison through water. It took nothing more than a handful of silver coins and sweet promises in a clerk's ear to cage a veteran captain outside his own citadel.

"Summon the duty officer of the main garrison," Garrett ordered, his voice dropping to a low, perilous rumble. "Now. Or by noon, I will have your name entered as an insubordinate obstructer of military command in a time of frontier crisis."

The sergeant's eyes wavered, unsettled by the raw fury in Garrett's gaze and the dried mire clinging to the border troops. After a tense silence, the man spat upon the cobbles.

"You may proceed to the west side-wing," the sergeant muttered sullenly. "As far as the outer receiving hall, no further. Mounts and pack beasts remain in the outer livery, and all civilian weapons are to be surrendered to the gatehouse lockbox. No steel enters without written clearance from the treasury."

Kael stiffened, his fingers instinctively clamping onto the hilt of his father's sword.

Two gate guards stepped forward with outstretched hands to disarm him. Kael looked up at Garrett, his breath coming hot and ragged with defiance.

Garrett looked down at Kael, his jaw clenched tight, and offered a slow, stern shake of his head. "Yield it, Kael. You are not facing timber wolves here."

A hot surge of indignity pounded in Kael's chest. Yet seeing the grim command in the captain's eyes, his grip slowly uncurled. He unbuckled his sword belt and surrendered his father's forged blade to the rough hands of the gatekeeper, who shoved it into an iron-banded chest and turned a heavy brass key.

With a squeal of chains, the iron portcullis rose halfway. Kael walked through the portal beside their pack mule, feeling his left hip suddenly hollow and bare.

***

The flags of the west corridor were slick with subterranean damp. Thick, cold masonry walls rose on either side, blackened with greasy soot from tallow braziers set into iron recesses. The harsh stink of sulfur, weapon oil, and wet lime replaced the clean scent of pine Kael had known all his life.

Garrett had departed down the hall alongside a duty officer to untangle their entry permits, leaving Kael, Mira, and Azariel seated in a vaulted stone alcove near the courtyard portal. Two sentries stood at attention outside the archway, halberds upright, watching them like prisoners under open arrest.

Kael sat stiffly upon a bench of rough-hewn oak. His left hand repeatedly brushed against his empty hip, searching for the familiar hilt that had accompanied him every single day. The void unnerved him. For seven seasons, Bran had forged him in the hills, and that weapon was his guard and part of his very self. Unarmed inside foreign stone walls, he felt stripped of his footing.

His mother had once spoken in passing that Bran had served as a soldier in this fortress before choosing peace in the valley. Yet looking at the insolent guards and the cold granite vaults, Kael could not fathom how his father had endured years in such a place.

"Sit quietly, Kael," Mira whispered softly from his side, setting her herbal satchel upon the flags. "You will only make those guards more suspicious if you keep balling your fists."

"They disarmed us like common thieves," Kael muttered, his teeth clenched tight.

Azariel sat motionless at the end of the bench. His hands rested upon his knees, his chin slightly lifted as he listened to the hollow tramp of boots echoing from the ramparts above. His features were pale, yet his calm gaze caught the dim lantern light with an uncanny, undisturbed stillness.

A low side door creaked open. A scullery boy in a fraying gray linen tunic hurried in, carrying a wooden tray bearing an earthenware jug and three crusts of dense rye bread. The boy's steps were skittish and trembling, his gaze glued to the stone floor, thoroughly intimidated by the armed guards in the passage.

As the boy moved to place the tray upon the stone table, his shoe caught on an uneven flagstone. The tray tipped, the jug rattled, and a round loaf tumbled to the floor, rolling to a stop against the toe of Azariel's boot.

The servant froze in terror. His face drained of color, and he dropped frantically to his knees to snatch it up, stammering apologies for his clumsiness.

Before the boy's trembling fingers could reach the bread, Azariel's hand lifted it from the stone.

Azariel blew away the stone dust from the crust with a gentle breath, exactly as Mira had taught him in the hearth kitchen. He looked up, extending the bread far too close to the lad's face.

Beneath the dim lantern light, Azariel's pupils reflected a faint, unearthly blue-white ember, cold and still.

"Thank you... for the water," Azariel said slowly. Each word fell measured and stiff, as though he were weighing the shape of every syllable upon his tongue.

The boy froze. His eyes widened in sheer horror at the pale light in Azariel's gaze and the unnatural closeness of the youth's face. Breath caught in the servant's throat as a sudden dread crept up his spine. With a panicked jerk, the lad snatched the bread from Azariel's palm, stumbled back two paces on trembling legs, and fled down the corridor without looking back.

Kael watched the boy disappear, his brow furrowing deep. Azariel did not grasp the mortal boundaries of space. His stiffness and the strange light in his eyes were not things that could be concealed if the castle watch began to take note.

A slow, deliberate tread echoed down the hall. Not the heavy clatter of iron sabatons, but the soft click of supple leather soles on smooth granite.

An elderly man entered the archway. He was lean and gaunt, yet carried himself with practiced dignity. He wore an austere tunic of black wool trimmed with fine silver thread along the high collar, and from his belt hung a heavy brass ring bearing a score of intricately notched keys. In his left hand, he carried a bundle wrapped in coarse canvas.

The two sentries outside the door snapped to rigid attention, grounding their halberds in sharp salute.

"That will do, lads," the old man said, his voice quiet yet carrying unchallenged authority. "Return to your posts in the east wing. I will see to Captain Garrett's company myself."

The guards bowed their heads and marched away without hesitation. The man in black stepped inside, drew the heavy oak door shut, and set the canvas bundle upon the stone table with a heavy metallic thud.

Kael rose instantly to his feet.

The elder drew back the canvas, revealing the forged broadsword of Bran alongside its leather belt. The weapon was pristine, undamaged.

"This was taken at the front gate," the old man said flatly, his gaze sweeping over them without the trace of a smile. "Gate wardens are extraordinarily fond of hoarding spoils without the slightest idea of what they hold."

His gaze dropped to the weapon's crossguard, studying the raised relief of a spear-headed lion stamped into the metal.

"The strike of the Pathfinders," the old man murmured softly. "Twenty winters ago, that frontier regiment was dissolved in the northern snows, and only the stubbornest fighters had the marrow to swing iron that heavy through the mountain rime. What is your name, lad?"

Kael swallowed hard, his heart hammering at the unit name he had never heard from his father's lips. "Kael. The sword belongs to my father, Bran."

The old man gave a slight, understanding nod. "Bran the Crag Hunter. The man who cast off his coat of mail to harvest wheat in the valley. It explains why Captain Garrett staked his commission to bring you through the portal."

"Who are you?" Kael asked, reaching out to reclaim the sword and buckling the belt firmly around his waist. The familiar, cold weight at his hip instantly grounded his footing.

"I am Tobias," the man replied tersely. "High Steward of this keep since His Grace's late father still commanded the frontier regiments."

Mira stepped forward a pace, her tone measured and wary. "Why would you return this blade to us, Master Tobias?"

Tobias met her gaze with cold, gray eyes. There was no warmth in his expression.

"Because the treasury seeks to muzzle your captain with paper snares," Tobias said quietly. "Chancellor Aldric is extraordinarily adept at using ancient protocols to purge whoever threatens his sway. I need Garrett's testimony before His Grace the Duke before Aldric succeeds in barring the council chamber."

Kael looked at the heavy brass key ring hanging at Tobias's waist, realizing that this old servant was maneuvering his own pieces behind the citadel's stones.

Tobias turned toward the door, resting a gaunt hand upon the iron latch. He glanced back, without looking at them directly.

"The side portal to the yard remains unbolted until the noon bell," Tobias whispered coldly. "Take the air in the courtyard if you have no wish to suffocate in this vault. And remember, I have said nothing to you."

The latch clinked as Tobias opened the door, stepping out into the shadows of the hall.

***

The inner courtyard spread wide beneath a pale, overcast sky. Paved with clean white gravel, it was flanked by limestone arcades that supported the tiered balconies of the ducal palace. High above along the battlements, guards marched in steady patrols, their plate armor glinting faintly as they passed without casting a glance below.

Azariel walked slowly across the crushed white stone.

With every stride he took, a faint tremor rose from the ground into the soles of his boots. The further he moved into the open yard, the harder that pulse throbbed behind his ribs, beating with a cold, unrelenting rhythm. The sharp taste of raw iron flooded the back of his mouth, as though he were chewing on frozen slag that had lain submerged in icy water for centuries.

At the center of the yard stood an ancient octagonal well. Its coping stones were carved from dense gray granite, grooved by the bite of iron chains and bucket ropes over endless generations. Brittle moss clung to the crevices of the masonry.

Azariel stopped before the well.

"Azariel?" Mira's voice came from just behind his shoulder, wary and hushed. "Do not lean too close if your senses are clouded."

Azariel did not answer. Drawn by an unseen pull, he pulled the coarse leather glove from his right hand. Slowly, he set his bare palm upon the damp, freezing rim of the well.

The air around him seemed to go dead and hollow.

The pulse surged into his flesh. It was not the white flame or the breathing warmth of Tebing Pinus, but an immense, rigid weight that chilled the blood. The vibration traveled up the bones of his wrist, into his shoulder, and struck the ember in his chest like an iron sledgehammer crashing down upon a freezing anvil.

Fathoms below the bedrock foundations, a rigid pulse beat with absolute regularity, cold, immovable, and rejecting mortal imperfection. It emanated from beneath the eastern foundations of the yard, pulling at the ember in his chest with an unyielding certainty.

His breath caught in his throat. The sinews of his jaw locked tight, and a faint sheen of pale blue light flared in his eyes before he forced his lids shut. His right hand, which had remained numb since the night at the waystation when he bolstered the pine shoots, trembled violently. A sudden frost bit into his fingertips, turning them white against the stone.

Azariel tore his hand away from the well, concealing his shaking fingers beneath the fold of his cloak.

Mira was at his side in an instant. Without a word, she caught his wrist, feeling his pulse hammer like a war drum. With practiced calm, she unstoppered a small vial of wild mint oil from her pouch, worked the tincture onto his skin, and began to massage his knuckles in firm, circular strokes.

Above on the parapet, the steady tramp of sentries echoed past without pausing.

"What is down there, El?" Mira whispered, keeping her head down so as not to draw the eyes of the watchmen.

Azariel drew a ragged breath, the cold air scraping his lungs. His voice came low and strained.

"It does not sleep, Mira," he murmured, his gaze fixed across the courtyard. "It is imprisoned... bound inside a space too narrow to let it breathe."

Kael closed in, stepping between them and the wall to shield Azariel from view. "In which direction?"

Azariel tilted his head toward the eastern corner of the citadel yard.

There, detached from the palace proper, stood a squat, windowless redoubt of dark ashlar stone. Its only entrance was a pair of massive iron-plated oak doors bolted with thick steel bars. Four armored guards wielding heavy halberds stood outside the threshold, motionless as bronze statues.

Kael narrowed his eyes, studying the fortification. "That building has no windows. Four halberdiers at the iron door... the vault where the house stores its greatest wealth."

"It lies behind those stones," Azariel whispered flatly. "In the depths of its foundation."

***

Night fell over Leovard with a freezing wind carrying the acrid stench of coal smoke from the lower city chimneys. From the citadel's main bell tower, the curfew bell tolled nine heavy, measured strokes. The sound rolled over the slate rooftops, commanding all citizens to douse their braziers and bar their doors.

On the upper floor of the Iron Lantern, a thick-walled stone tavern tucked beneath the inner ramparts, a lone tallow candle burned upon a three-legged oak table.

The chamber door creaked open as Garrett stepped through. The captain looked utterly spent. He had unbuckled his gorget and iron gauntlets, revealing the deep lines carved into his brow and the dust clinging to his graying beard. He unbelted his broadsword, laid it upon the table, and sank heavily into a chair with an exhausted sigh.

"What news from the council, Captain?" Kael asked, leaning near the narrow embrasure window.

Garrett rubbed his rough face with both palms. "The hearing is stayed. Aldric filed a formal written objection against my report. He claims I abandoned my frontier post without written authorization from the garrison marshal, and demands that the trade guild's inventory of northern losses be heard first by the Duke."

"Vane?" Kael's voice was sharp.

"Feasting councilors and treasury clerks at his table in the High Quarter," Garrett said bitterly. "Pouring his finest vintage into their cups, whispering that the border valley is better leased out to a merchant cartel than defended with garrison blood. Our audience is pushed back two full days."

Mira set a steaming mug of steeped herbs before him, meeting the captain's gaze with steady resolve. "Two days confined to this room while they twist the truth in the council chamber? Are we merely to wait until the doors are bolted from the inside?"

Garrett looked down at the mug before nodding heavily. "Were it not for Tobias speaking to the gate warden, we would be sitting on wet straw in the garrison lockup tonight. Tobias vouched for this room under the authority of the ducal household, but he demands a return: he wants my testimony before the Duke to break Aldric's sway. His warning was stern: stay away from the east wing unless I walk beside you."

"Because Aldric has us watched?" Kael asked.

"Because Aldric is a wolf wrapped in parchment," Garrett replied flatly. "He does not use cold steel to kill his enemies. He uses wax seals and confiscation rolls."

Garrett drained the herbal brew, set the mug down, and rose to his feet. "I must see to our mounts in the stables and speak with an old sergeant who still owes me a debt. Bolt the door from the inside. Open it for no one unless I knock thrice."

Once the heavy thud of Garrett's boots faded down the timber stair, silence reclaimed the cramped chamber.

Mira sat on the straw pallet beside Azariel, wrapping clean linen steeped in soothing oils around the youth's right wrist. Azariel leaned back against the cold masonry, his gaze fixed upon the candle flame fluttering in the draft that slipped through the stone wall.

Kael walked to the narrow slit in the wall. The freezing night air brushed his cheeks, smelling of damp moss and old iron from the distant yard.

Through the narrow embrasure, Kael could see the squat silhouette of the eastern stone vault rising dark against the ramparts. The building stood quiet in the night, yet directly above its iron-plated doors, an iron lantern burned with an unblinking, yellow glow. Two sentries stood motionless beneath its light, their halberd blades glinting pale against the dark stone.

Kael looked down at his father's broadsword propped against the stone sill.

His thumb touched the pommel. At the outer gate this morning, the wardens had stripped this weapon from him with nothing more than a single command and a sheet of fresh ink. All his life in the valley, Bran had taught him that courage, swift steel, and an unyielding stance were everything a man needed to protect the ones he loved. If a beast came, strike its throat. If danger drew near, plant your feet and hold the line.

Yet inside this stone city, forged steel felt dull and useless.

The enemy they faced tonight possessed neither fangs nor claws. Their enemy hid behind the sweet smile of a vintner draped in purple wool, behind stacks of parchment tended by tired-eyed clerks, and behind an iron door sealed with wax that no man could touch without an official scroll. His father's sword could not cut through an order of arrest, and his hunter's reflexes could not turn the brass keys hanging from a chamberlain's belt.

Kael gripped the sword's hilt tightly, feeling the cold steel bite into his palm.

Slowly, his fingers loosened. He let go of the leather-wrapped grip, letting his hands fall empty at his sides. This time, he did not take it back.

Outside the window, the night wind surged across the slate roofs. In the silent courtyard below, the iron lantern above the treasury doors flickered once, its flame bowing hard against the gale before righting itself, burning steady, rigid, and cold against the black granite stone.

Kael watched that pale flame for a quiet moment, then drew the rough woolen curtain shut, sealing out the night.
